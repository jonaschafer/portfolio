How to play

The goal

Move all 15 of your checkers around the board, into your home board, and then off. First one to get all 15 off wins.

You’re cream. Brown is the computer.

The board

24 triangles, called points, numbered 1 to 24 from your side. The numbers are printed on the frame.

  • Your home board is points 1 to 6, bottom right.
  • Brown’s home board is points 19 to 24, top right.
  • The bar is the strip down the middle. Checkers that get hit wait there.
  • The tray on the right holds checkers that have been borne off.

You move from 24 down to 1: right to left across the top, then left to right across the bottom, then off. Brown goes the opposite way.

Starting position

Each side has 2 checkers on its 24-point, 5 on its 13 (the midpoint), 3 on its 8 and 5 on its 6. Brown’s setup is the mirror image of yours.

Who goes first

Each player rolls one die. Higher die goes first and plays the two numbers showing as their opening move. Ties roll again.

Moving

Roll two dice. Each die is a separate move. You can move one checker for both numbers or two checkers one number each.

A checker can land on any point that’s empty, has your own checkers, or has exactly one Brown checker. It can’t land on a point with two or more Brown checkers.

When one checker plays both dice, the stop in between has to be open too.

On this board: click a checker, then click where it should go. Gold dots show every legal spot, including spots that use both dice.

Doubles

Roll the same number on both dice and you play that number four times. 6-6 means four moves of 6.

Blocked points and using both dice

A point with two or more of the other side’s checkers is blocked.

You must use both dice if there’s any legal way to. If only one die can be played, play it. If either one could be played but not both, you must play the larger. If nothing’s legal, you lose your turn.

This is why the board sometimes won’t let a checker move even though the spot looks open: that move would make your other die unplayable.

Hitting and the bar

A single checker alone on a point is a blot. If the other side lands on it, the blot is hit: it goes to the bar and has to start over from the far end.

Hitting is a big swing. A checker hit on your 6-point loses 19 pips of progress.

Coming in from the bar

If you have a checker on the bar, you can’t move anything else until it comes back in. It enters in Brown’s home board: a 1 enters on your 24-point, a 2 on 23, up to a 6 on 19.

If the point for both numbers is blocked, you can’t enter and you lose the turn. If Brown has all six home points made, you’re closed out until one opens up.

Bearing off

Once all 15 of your checkers are in your home board (1 to 6), you can start taking them off. A 4 takes a checker off the 4-point.

If you roll higher than your highest checker, take off from the highest point. You can always use a number to move inside your home board instead.

If one of your checkers gets hit while you’re bearing off, it has to come all the way back around before you can bear off again.

Winning

  • Single game (1 point): you bear off all 15 first.
  • Gammon (2 points): Brown hasn’t borne off any checkers yet.
  • Backgammon (3 points): Brown hasn’t borne off any and still has a checker on the bar or in your home board.

The doubling cube

Real matches use a cube to raise the stakes: before rolling, a player can offer to double. The other side either accepts and plays for twice the points, or concedes. This trainer leaves the cube out so you can focus on the checker play.

Strategy basics

Pip count

Your pip count is the total distance your checkers still have to travel: each checker’s point number added up (a checker on the bar counts 25). Both sides start at 167. Lower is better. It tells you who’s ahead in a race.

Blots and shots

Every blot is a risk. Count the shots: how many of Brown’s 36 possible rolls would hit it. A blot 6 or fewer points away from a Brown checker is a direct shot (11+ rolls). 7 to 12 away needs a combination and is much harder to hit.

With “Show danger” on, red numbers on your blots show exactly that count.

Making points

Two checkers on a point make it yours: safe from hits and blocked for Brown. The best ones to own early are your 5-point, then your 4-point and your bar point (7). The 5-point is the best point on the board.

Anchors

A point you hold in Brown’s home board is an anchor. It’s a safe landing spot when you’re hit and keeps Brown from closing its board. Brown’s 5-point (your 20) is the best anchor.

Primes

Several made points in a row form a prime. Six in a row is a full prime: nothing behind it can get past. Build primes in front of Brown’s back checkers, and get your own back checkers out before Brown builds one.

The race

Once every checker has passed the other side’s, there’s no more hitting. It’s a race. Bring checkers home efficiently, avoid wasting pips, and bear off as fast as you can.

Opening moves

Every first roll has a standard move that good players agree on. The coach marks it “Standard” when you play it.

They mostly do one of two things: put two checkers on the same point so Brown can’t land there, or move one checker from 24 so your two back checkers aren’t stuck together.

Reading moves

13/8 means a checker moved from point 13 to point 8. 6/5* means it hit on 5. bar/22 is entering from the bar, 6/off is bearing off, and 13/7(2) means two checkers made the same move.
